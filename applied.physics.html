UNIT 1

Fundamentals of Quantum Mechanics

1.1 Introduction to Quantum Mechanics

Quantum mechanics is the branch of physics that describes the behaviour of matter and energy at atomic and subatomic scales. It is used to explain phenomena that classical physics cannot describe accurately, such as atomic spectra and the behaviour of electrons in atoms.

1.2 Wave-Particle Duality

Wave-particle duality states that microscopic particles, such as electrons and photons, can exhibit both wave-like and particle-like properties depending on the experiment.

Light shows wave behaviour through interference and diffraction, while the photoelectric effect demonstrates its particle nature. Electrons can also produce diffraction patterns.

de Broglie wavelength: λ = h / p = h / mv

Where:

  • λ = de Broglie wavelength
  • h = Planck's constant
  • p = momentum of the particle
  • m = mass of the particle
  • v = velocity of the particle
Example: An electron behaves like a particle when it transfers energy in a collision, but it can also show wave-like diffraction when passing through a suitable crystal.

1.3 Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle

The Heisenberg uncertainty principle states that the position and momentum of a microscopic particle cannot both be known with unlimited precision at the same time.

Δx × Δp ≥ ℏ / 2

Here, Δx is uncertainty in position, Δp is uncertainty in momentum, and ℏ = h / 2π is the reduced Planck constant.

Increasing the precision of position measurement increases the minimum possible uncertainty in momentum, and vice versa.

1.4 Wave Function

A wave function, commonly represented by ψ (psi), is a mathematical function used to describe the quantum state of a particle. It contains information about the possible outcomes of measurements.

The wave function itself is generally not directly observable. According to the Born interpretation, |ψ|² represents the probability density of finding a particle at a particular position.

Probability density = |ψ|²

1.5 Schrödinger Wave Equation

The Schrödinger equation is a fundamental equation of quantum mechanics. It describes how the quantum state of a system changes and helps determine the allowed energy states of a particle.

Time-dependent equation:
iℏ (∂ψ / ∂t) = Ĥψ
One-dimensional time-independent equation:
-(ℏ² / 2m)(d²ψ / dx²) + Vψ = Eψ

Where:

  • i = imaginary unit
  • ℏ = reduced Planck constant
  • m = mass of the particle
  • V = potential energy
  • E = total energy eigenvalue
  • ψ = wave function

1.6 Particle in a One-Dimensional Box

A particle in a one-dimensional box is an idealised quantum system in which a particle is confined between two infinitely high potential walls separated by a distance L.

The particle cannot have arbitrary energy. Only certain discrete energy values are allowed. This is called energy quantisation.

Allowed energy levels:
Eₙ = n²h² / 8mL²

Where n = 1, 2, 3, ... is the quantum number.

Example: If the quantum number changes from n = 1 to n = 2, the energy becomes four times the ground-state energy, because energy is proportional to n².

1.7 Applications of Quantum Mechanics

  • Understanding atomic structure and atomic spectra.
  • Design of semiconductor devices and transistors.
  • Development of lasers and quantum sensors.
  • Understanding chemical bonding and material properties.
  • Development of quantum computers.
UNIT 2

Quantum Theory of Solids

2.1 Introduction to Solids

Solids are materials in which atoms or molecules are closely packed. Their electrical and thermal properties depend on their atomic arrangement and the behaviour of electrons.

Quantum theory helps explain why different solids behave as conductors, semiconductors or insulators.

2.2 Energy Bands in Solids

When many atoms come together to form a solid, their individual energy levels split and form closely spaced energy bands.

  • Valence band: The highest energy band that is normally occupied by electrons at low temperature.
  • Conduction band: The energy band in which electrons can move through the material and contribute to electrical conduction.
  • Forbidden energy gap: The energy range between the valence band and conduction band in which no allowed electron states exist in the ideal band model.

2.3 Classification of Materials

Conductors

Conductors allow electric current to flow easily. In metals, the valence and conduction bands overlap or a band is partially filled. Copper and aluminium are common examples.

Insulators

Insulators have a large energy gap between the valence band and conduction band. Very few electrons can reach the conduction band under ordinary conditions. Glass and rubber are examples.

Semiconductors

Semiconductors have a relatively small energy gap. Their electrical conductivity can be controlled by temperature, light and doping. Silicon and germanium are common examples.

Example: Silicon is used in computer chips because its conductivity can be controlled by adding suitable impurities and applying electric fields.

2.4 Fermi Energy and Fermi Level

Fermi energy is the energy of the highest occupied electron state at absolute zero temperature. The Fermi level is an important reference energy used to describe electron occupancy in solids.

The position of the Fermi level helps explain the electrical behaviour of conductors, semiconductors and insulators.

2.5 Fermi-Dirac Distribution

The Fermi-Dirac distribution describes the probability that an available electron state of energy E is occupied by an electron at temperature T.

f(E) = 1 / [1 + exp((E - Eᶠ) / kT)]

Where:

  • E = energy of the state
  • Eᶠ = Fermi energy or Fermi level in the relevant context
  • k = Boltzmann constant
  • T = absolute temperature in kelvin

2.6 Intrinsic and Extrinsic Semiconductors

An intrinsic semiconductor is a pure semiconductor without intentionally added impurity atoms. In it, the number of free electrons equals the number of holes at thermal equilibrium.

An extrinsic semiconductor is formed by adding controlled impurities to a pure semiconductor. This process is called doping.

N-type Semiconductor

An N-type semiconductor is formed by doping silicon or germanium with a donor impurity, commonly a pentavalent element. Electrons become the majority charge carriers.

P-type Semiconductor

A P-type semiconductor is formed by doping with an acceptor impurity, commonly a trivalent element. Holes become the majority charge carriers.

Remember: N-type does not mean the material has an overall negative charge. P-type does not mean it has an overall positive charge. Both materials are electrically neutral overall.

2.7 Applications of Semiconductor Physics

  • Diodes and rectifiers.
  • Transistors and integrated circuits.
  • LEDs and solar cells.
  • Microprocessors and memory devices.
  • Sensors and communication systems.
UNIT 3

Quantum Computing and Communication

3.1 Introduction to Quantum Computing

Quantum computing is a field of computing that uses principles of quantum mechanics to process information. Unlike classical computers, which use bits, quantum computers use quantum bits called qubits.

3.2 Classical Bit and Qubit

A classical bit has one definite value at a time: either 0 or 1. A qubit is a quantum system that can be represented as a superposition of the basis states |0⟩ and |1⟩.

General qubit state:
|ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩

Here α and β are complex probability amplitudes, and they satisfy:

|α|² + |β|² = 1

3.3 Superposition

Superposition means that a quantum system can be in a combination of different possible states before measurement. When a qubit is measured in the computational basis, the outcome is 0 or 1, with probabilities determined by its amplitudes.

Example: A qubit in the state (|0⟩ + |1⟩) / √2 has equal probability of being measured as 0 or 1 in the computational basis.

3.4 Quantum Entanglement

Entanglement is a quantum phenomenon in which two or more particles share a joint quantum state. Their measurement outcomes can be correlated in ways that cannot be explained by classical independent states.

Entanglement is important in quantum communication, quantum teleportation protocols and quantum information science.

3.5 Quantum Gates

Quantum gates are operations that change the state of qubits. They are the basic building blocks of quantum circuits.

  • Pauli-X gate: Similar to a NOT operation on computational basis states; it swaps |0⟩ and |1⟩.
  • Pauli-Y gate: A single-qubit operation that changes amplitudes and phases.
  • Pauli-Z gate: Changes the phase of the |1⟩ component while leaving |0⟩ unchanged.
  • Hadamard gate: Creates an equal superposition from a computational basis state.
  • CNOT gate: A two-qubit gate that flips the target qubit when the control qubit is 1.

3.6 Quantum Measurement

Measurement converts quantum information into a classical result. For a qubit measured in the computational basis, the result is either 0 or 1. The probability of each result depends on the quantum state before measurement.

3.7 Quantum Communication

Quantum communication uses quantum states to transmit or share information. It includes techniques such as quantum key distribution and entanglement-based communication.

Quantum Key Distribution (QKD)

QKD is a method for establishing a shared cryptographic key using quantum states. In ideal protocols, attempts to measure transmitted quantum states can introduce detectable disturbances.

3.8 Applications and Challenges

Applications

  • Research into quantum chemistry and material simulation.
  • Some specialised optimisation and mathematical problems.
  • Quantum communication and secure key distribution.
  • Research in advanced sensing and measurement.

Challenges

  • Qubits are sensitive to environmental noise.
  • Quantum states can lose coherence.
  • Error correction requires significant resources.
  • Building and maintaining quantum hardware is difficult.
Quantum computers are not faster than classical computers for every task. Their advantage depends on the problem and the algorithm being used.
UNIT 4

Photonics: Fibre Optics and Lasers

4.1 Introduction to Photonics

Photonics is the science and technology of generating, controlling and detecting light. It has applications in optical communication, medical instruments, imaging, sensors and data transmission.

4.2 Optical Fibre

An optical fibre is a thin, flexible transparent strand made from glass or plastic that carries light signals from one place to another.

An optical fibre mainly consists of:

  • Core: The central region through which light travels.
  • Cladding: The surrounding layer with a lower refractive index than the core.
  • Protective coating: Protects the fibre from physical damage and environmental effects.

4.3 Total Internal Reflection

Total internal reflection occurs when light travels from an optically denser medium to a less dense medium and the angle of incidence is greater than the critical angle.

Optical fibres use this principle to guide light along the core.

Critical angle: sin C = n₂ / n₁

This relation applies when n₁ is greater than n₂, where n₁ is the refractive index of the denser medium and n₂ is that of the less dense medium.

4.4 Numerical Aperture

Numerical aperture (NA) describes the light-gathering ability of an optical fibre. For a step-index fibre in air:

NA = √(n₁² - n₂²)

Here n₁ is the core refractive index and n₂ is the cladding refractive index, with n₁ greater than n₂.

Example: If the refractive indices of the core and cladding are 1.50 and 1.48 respectively:

NA = √(1.50² - 1.48²)
NA = √(2.25 - 2.1904)
NA = √0.0596 ≈ 0.244

4.5 Types of Optical Fibres

  • Single-mode fibre: Supports one principal propagation mode and is commonly used for long-distance, high-bandwidth communication.
  • Multimode fibre: Supports multiple propagation modes and is commonly used for shorter-distance links.
  • Step-index fibre: Has an abrupt change in refractive index between core and cladding.
  • Graded-index fibre: Has a core refractive index that gradually decreases from the centre outward.

4.6 Optical Fibre Losses

Signal power decreases as light travels through an optical fibre. This reduction is called attenuation.

  • Absorption loss: Some optical energy is absorbed by the fibre material or impurities.
  • Scattering loss: Light is scattered due to microscopic variations in the material.
  • Bending loss: Light escapes when the fibre is bent beyond suitable limits.

4.7 LASER

LASER stands for Light Amplification by Stimulated Emission of Radiation. A laser produces a highly directional and coherent beam of light, often with a narrow range of wavelengths.

4.8 Spontaneous and Stimulated Emission

Spontaneous emission: An excited atom or system returns to a lower energy state and emits a photon without an external photon triggering the transition.

Stimulated emission: An incoming photon can stimulate an excited system to emit another photon with matching frequency, phase, direction and polarisation in the idealised description.

4.9 Population Inversion

Population inversion is a condition in which more particles occupy an upper energy state than a lower energy state involved in the laser transition. It is essential for achieving optical gain through stimulated emission.

4.10 Properties of Laser Light

  • Coherence: Laser light has a strong phase relationship across the beam.
  • Directionality: It can travel as a narrow, highly directed beam.
  • Monochromaticity: It often has a narrow spectral width.
  • High intensity: Energy can be concentrated into a small area.

4.11 Applications of Lasers

  • Optical fibre communication.
  • Industrial cutting and precision processing.
  • Medical and scientific instruments.
  • Barcode scanners and optical storage systems.
  • Scientific measurement and research.
UNIT 5

Superconductors and Nanomaterials

5.1 Introduction to Superconductivity

Superconductivity is a physical phenomenon in which certain materials, when cooled below a characteristic critical temperature, exhibit zero electrical resistance and expel magnetic fields from their interior under suitable conditions.

5.2 Critical Temperature

Critical temperature (Tc) is the temperature below which a material enters its superconducting state, provided the magnetic field and current are within the material's critical limits.

5.3 Meissner Effect

The Meissner effect is the expulsion of magnetic flux from the interior of a superconducting material as it enters the superconducting state.

This is a defining property of superconductivity and distinguishes a superconductor from an ordinary perfect conductor.

5.4 Types of Superconductors

Type-I Superconductors

Type-I superconductors generally show complete magnetic flux expulsion below a single critical magnetic field. Many elemental superconductors belong to this category.

Type-II Superconductors

Type-II superconductors have two critical magnetic fields. Between these fields, magnetic flux can enter in quantised vortices. Many technologically useful superconductors belong to this category.

5.5 BCS Theory

BCS theory, named after Bardeen, Cooper and Schrieffer, explains conventional superconductivity. In this theory, electrons form correlated pairs called Cooper pairs through an effective interaction involving the crystal lattice.

These pairs collectively form a coherent quantum state that accounts for superconducting behaviour in conventional superconductors.

5.6 Applications of Superconductors

  • MRI and NMR systems.
  • Powerful electromagnets.
  • Particle accelerators.
  • Magnetic levitation research and transport systems.
  • Superconducting quantum circuits.

5.7 Introduction to Nanomaterials

Nanomaterials are materials that have at least one dimension approximately within the nanoscale range, commonly 1 to 100 nanometres.

At this scale, materials can show properties different from their bulk forms because of their large surface-area-to-volume ratio and quantum-size effects.

5.8 Classification of Nanomaterials

  • Zero-dimensional: All three dimensions are in the nanoscale range. Example: quantum dots.
  • One-dimensional: Two dimensions are nanoscale while one dimension is larger. Example: nanowires.
  • Two-dimensional: One dimension is nanoscale. Example: thin films and graphene sheets.
  • Three-dimensional nanostructured materials: Bulk materials containing nanoscale structural features.

5.9 Properties of Nanomaterials

  • High surface-area-to-volume ratio.
  • Size-dependent optical properties.
  • Changes in electrical and magnetic behaviour.
  • Different mechanical and chemical properties.
  • Quantum confinement in suitable nanoscale systems.

5.10 Examples and Applications

Graphene

Graphene is a single layer of carbon atoms arranged in a two-dimensional honeycomb lattice. It is known for its high electrical conductivity, strength and flexibility.

Carbon Nanotubes

Carbon nanotubes are cylindrical nanostructures made from carbon atoms. They are studied for their electrical, thermal and mechanical properties.

Quantum Dots

Quantum dots are semiconductor nanocrystals whose optical properties depend on their size. They are used in research, displays and imaging technologies.

Applications of Nanotechnology

  • Electronics and advanced sensors.
  • Solar cells and energy storage research.
  • Drug delivery and biomedical imaging research.
  • Protective coatings and advanced materials.
  • Water purification and environmental technologies.

Practice Questions with Answers

Try answering each question before revealing its answer.

Q1. What is wave-particle duality?

It is the principle that microscopic objects such as electrons and photons can exhibit both wave-like and particle-like behaviour.

Q2. State Heisenberg's uncertainty principle.

Position and momentum cannot both be known with unlimited precision. Their uncertainties satisfy Δx Δp ≥ ℏ / 2.

Q3. What does the square of the wave function represent?

|ψ|² represents the probability density of finding a particle at a particular position.

Q4. What is the forbidden energy gap?

It is the energy range between the valence band and conduction band in which no allowed electron states exist in the ideal band model.

Q5. Differentiate between intrinsic and extrinsic semiconductors.

An intrinsic semiconductor is pure, while an extrinsic semiconductor has intentionally added impurities through doping to control its electrical properties.

Q6. What is a qubit?

A qubit is the basic unit of quantum information. Its state can be a superposition of the basis states |0⟩ and |1⟩.

Q7. Define quantum entanglement.

It is a quantum phenomenon in which two or more systems share a joint state and can show correlations that have no classical counterpart.

Q8. What is total internal reflection?

It occurs when light travels from a denser to a less dense optical medium and the angle of incidence exceeds the critical angle.

Q9. What is population inversion?

It is the condition in which the upper laser energy level has a greater population than the lower level involved in the laser transition.

Q10. Explain the Meissner effect.

It is the expulsion of magnetic flux from the interior of a material when it enters the superconducting state under suitable conditions.

Q11. What are nanomaterials?

Nanomaterials have at least one dimension approximately between 1 and 100 nanometres and can show size-dependent properties.

Q12. Give two applications of optical fibres.

Optical fibres are used in high-speed communication networks and medical imaging instruments such as endoscopes.

Numerical Practice

Q13. If a particle's momentum is doubled, what happens to its de Broglie wavelength?

Since λ = h / p, doubling momentum makes the de Broglie wavelength half its original value.

Q14. If the length of a one-dimensional box is doubled, what happens to the energy of a fixed quantum state?

Since Eₙ is proportional to 1 / L², doubling L makes the energy one-fourth of its original value.

Q15. A qubit has α = 1/√2 and β = 1/√2. What are the measurement probabilities?

Probability of 0 = |α|² = 1/2 = 50%. Probability of 1 = |β|² = 1/2 = 50%.
